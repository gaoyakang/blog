## 08-22

08-22 18:50 - 21:05 Today, I went for training again, still with a kickboard and air pocket. The coach asked me to add the action of holding water when paddling. During practice, I realized my misconception from the previous days of training (I thought my hands were straight). The main correction was that when breathing on the right side, my left hand did not move straight forward but unconsciously leaned towards the midline of my body. I always thought my hands were moving straight forward. After asking the coach to record a video, I saw that there was indeed a problem. I have been practicing this since then, consciously leaning to the left a bit more in the direction I think is correct. This way, I don't have to raise my head to take a breath when breathing, and I can breathe through my arms. To be honest, today I felt like I was getting worse with practice. Fortunately, after recording a video later, I found the mistake and suddenly realized that the shoulder rotation on the left side is the same as that on the right side. Copying the shoulder rotation on the left side to the right side also helped me breathe better. Instead of the body rotating, it's the shoulder rotation that drives the body to rotate. I was wondering why I felt my hands were straight but were actually leaning to the right. My speculation is: because of the injury to my right knee, the nervous system silently re-labeled "center of gravity leaning to the left" as "center of gravity". In the water, buoyancy cuts out most of the influence of gravity, making it easier for the body to return to the anatomical neutral position (spine vertical, shoulders horizontal). But the problem is: my brain still carries the reference of "leaning to the left = center of gravity" from land. So when I think my left hand is "straight forward", I am actually referencing that shifted reference point - relative to the true midline in the water, my hand is leaning to the right. In addition, the freestyle roll is a dynamic chain: right leg kick → right hip rotation upward → left shoulder downward pressure → left hand extending forward. If the right leg is unable to exert force or cannot exert force due to injury, the amplitude and strength of the right roll will decrease.
In order to maintain balance and propel forward, the body will instinctively allow the left hand to cross the midline to "reach" for the water on the right side - equivalent to compensating for the lack of power on the right side by extending the left hand forward. This is a very subtle compensatory pattern that is difficult to detect, as the brain perceives it as "normal exertion of force"
